--==# Introduction #==--

    Aesop is an ancient teller of short tales, and this little book gathers ten of the most beloved ones.
    In these pages you will meet clever foxes, proud lions, a patient tortoise, and a boastful hare. Each animal learns something the hard way.
    Every tale is short, and every tale ends with a moral, so the lesson stays with you long after the story is done.

--==# The Fox and the Grapes #==--

    One warm afternoon a hungry fox trotted through an orchard and spotted a bunch of ripe grapes hanging high on a vine. The fox licked his lips, for the fruit looked sweet and juicy.
    The fox crouched low and jumped, but the grapes hung just out of reach. Again and again the fox leaped, snapping at the air, yet each time he fell back with empty jaws. At last the fox grew tired and his legs ached.
    Lifting his nose in the air, the fox walked away. "I did not want those grapes anyway," he sniffed. "They are surely sour and not worth my trouble."
    Moral: It is easy to despise what you cannot have.

--==# The Tortoise and the Hare #==--

    A hare was always boasting about how fast he could run. Tired of his bragging, a slow tortoise challenged him to a race, and every animal in the meadow gathered to watch.
    The hare sprang ahead and was soon so far in front that he decided to nap beneath a shady tree. The tortoise, however, never stopped. Step by steady step he plodded past the sleeping hare.
    When the hare awoke and dashed to the finish, it was too late. The tortoise had already crossed the line, and the crowd cheered for the patient winner.
    Moral: Slow and steady wins the race.

--==# The Lion and the Mouse #==--

    A great lion lay sleeping when a tiny mouse ran across his paw. The lion woke with a roar and trapped the little creature. "Spare me," squeaked the mouse, "and one day I may repay your kindness." The lion laughed at the thought but let him go.
    Not long after, the lion was caught in a hunter's net. He roared and struggled, yet the ropes held fast. Hearing the noise, the mouse hurried over and gnawed through the cords until the lion was free.
    Moral: No act of kindness is ever wasted.

--==# The North Wind and the Sun #==--

    The North Wind and the Sun argued about which of them was stronger. To settle the matter they agreed on a test: whoever could make a passing traveler remove his cloak would be the winner.
    The North Wind blew first, cold and fierce. But the harder he blew, the tighter the traveler pulled his cloak around him. At last the wind gave up, worn out and beaten.
    Then the Sun shone gently, warming the air. Soon the traveler grew hot, and of his own accord he took off his cloak and rested in the shade.
    Moral: Gentleness often succeeds where force fails.

--==# The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse #==--

    A town mouse once went on a journey to visit his cousin, who lived at the edge of a wide field in the country. The country mouse was poor in goods but generous in spirit, and he laid out the very best that his small store could offer: hard grains of wheat and barley, a few dry roots, a nibbled crust of dark bread, and a cup of cold water drawn from the stream. The town mouse tasted a little of this and a little of that, wrinkled his nose, and pushed the plate away with a sigh.
    "My dear cousin," he said, "how can you bear to live on such plain fare, in such a quiet and lonely place? Nothing ever happens here. The days go by one after another, each the same as the last, with no music, no company, and no delight. You are wasting your life among these hedgerows. Come home with me to the city, and I will show you what living really means. My house stands in a great pantry, and every evening the tables are crowded with cheese and honey, figs and dates, cakes and cream, more than a hundred mice could finish in a year."
    The country mouse allowed himself to be persuaded, and the two set off together across the fields and along the dusty roads, arriving at the great house late in the night. It was all exactly as the town mouse had promised. In the dining hall the remains of a splendid feast still covered the long table, and the two mice climbed up among the silver dishes and began upon the finest supper the country mouse had ever seen. He tasted rich cheese and sweet fig paste, and he was just lifting a crumb of honey cake when the doors burst open and a party of servants came in to clear the table, laughing and talking in loud voices.
    The two mice fled to a crack in the wall and pressed themselves flat in the dark, hearts drumming, until the servants had gone. "That is nothing," whispered the town mouse. "They never stay long. Come back and try the cream." But hardly had they crept out again when a great dog barked in the passage, so near and so suddenly that the mice tumbled over each other to reach the crack once more. When at last the house fell silent, the country mouse gathered up his hat and his stick and turned toward the door.
    "You may keep your fine table and your grand hall," he said, bowing to his cousin. "The food is rich, I grant you, and there is more of it than I shall see in all my seasons. But you pay for every mouthful with a fright, and a dinner eaten in fear is no dinner at all. I am going home to my field, where the fare is plain but the peace is certain. There I can finish my barley in comfort, with no dog at the door and no servant at the lamp, and sleep the whole night through under my own quiet hedge."
    He did not leave at once, however, for the town mouse begged him to stay one more day and see the city by lamplight. "You have only seen the pantry in its worst hour," he said. "Wait until the household sleeps, and you will understand why no sensible mouse would ever live anywhere else." So the country mouse, not wishing to seem ungrateful, agreed to stay until the following night, though he kept his hat within reach and his eye upon the crack in the wall.
    The next evening began well enough. The servants banked the fires and went up to their beds, the great house grew still, and the two mice came out upon the table like lords walking their own estate. The town mouse showed his cousin the proper way to open a fig, the corner of the cheese where the taste ran sweetest, and the dish of cream that had been left uncovered beside the window. For a little while the country mouse forgot his fright, and thought that perhaps his cousin had been right after all, and that a bold mouse might live like a king on what a rich house threw away.
    Then, without so much as a creak of warning, a shadow dropped from the window seat onto the table, soft as dust and swift as water. It was the house cat, who had been watching the cream dish for an hour with half-closed eyes. The town mouse shrieked and dove between the candlesticks; the country mouse went over the table edge with a crash among the spoons, ran the length of the hall with the cat's breath at his tail, and did not stop running until he had put two closed doors and a cellar stair between himself and those green eyes. There he sat in the dark a long time, listening to his own heart, and thinking of his hedge at home, and of the stream, and of the barley that had never once tried to eat him.
    When morning came he would not be persuaded to stay another hour. The town mouse walked with him as far as the garden gate and pressed a piece of cheese upon him for the road, still protesting that one bad night in ten was a small rent to pay for such a table. The country mouse thanked him kindly, but shook his head. "Cousin, we count differently," he said. "You reckon the price of your feasts in frights and escapes, and find the bargain good. I reckon the worth of a meal by how quietly I may finish it. By my count, the poorest supper in my field is richer than the finest here, for I own every minute of it, and no cat holds the lease."
    So the country mouse walked home under the stars, well content with roots and water ever after; and the town mouse, for all his figs and honey, never once knew a meal without listening for footsteps.
    Moral: A quiet life in safety is better than luxury lived in fear.

--==# The Ant and the Grasshopper #==--

    All summer long a grasshopper sang and danced in the warm sun, while a busy ant toiled to and fro, carrying grain to her nest. "Why work so hard?" laughed the grasshopper. "Come and play." The ant only kept on gathering her store.
    When winter came the fields were bare and cold. The grasshopper, hungry and shivering, crept to the ant's door to beg for food. But the ant had only enough for her own family, and the grasshopper was left to regret his idle days.
    Moral: Prepare today for the needs of tomorrow.

--==# The Crow and the Pitcher #==--

    A thirsty crow found a pitcher with a little water at the bottom. She stretched her beak down as far as she could, but the water lay too low to reach, and her thirst grew worse with every try.
    The clever crow thought for a moment. Then, one by one, she began dropping small pebbles into the pitcher. With each stone the water rose a little higher, until at last it reached the brim and she drank her fill and cawed with delight.
    Moral: Patient thinking solves what force cannot.

--==# The Dog and His Reflection #==--

    A dog was trotting home with a fine bone in his mouth when he crossed a narrow bridge over a still pond. Looking down, he saw his own reflection in the water and mistook it for another dog carrying a bone.
    Greedy for more, he snapped at the other dog to snatch its prize. But the moment he opened his jaws, his own bone tumbled into the pond and sank out of sight. The foolish dog was left with nothing at all.
    Moral: Grasp at too much and you may lose what you have.

--==# The Goose and the Golden Eggs #==--

    A farmer and his wife owned a goose that laid one shining egg of solid gold every single morning. Little by little the eggs made them rich, yet the more gold they gathered, the more impatient they became.
    "Why wait each day for a single egg?" they said. "Inside the goose there must be a great heap of gold." So they killed the goose to take it all at once. But when they looked inside, it was just like any other goose, and their golden eggs were gone forever.
    Moral: Greed for more can destroy the good you already have.

--==# The Boy Who Cried Wolf #==--

    A shepherd boy grew bored while watching his flock on the hillside. For a bit of fun he shouted, "Wolf! Wolf!" The villagers came running to help, only to find the boy laughing. He played the same trick a second time, and again they came for nothing.
    Then one day a real wolf crept among the sheep. The boy cried out in terror, "Wolf! Wolf!" But this time the villagers thought it was another joke, and no one came. The wolf scattered the helpless flock.
    Moral: A liar is not believed even when he tells the truth.
